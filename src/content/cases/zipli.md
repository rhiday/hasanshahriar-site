Food waste is easy to agree with and surprisingly hard to operate.

Inside a restaurant, surplus happens at the worst possible moment for software: during service, when the team is busy, the food is changing quickly and nobody wants another administrative system. Outside the restaurant, managers still need reliable data, reporting and a way to understand what is happening across locations.

Zipli is our attempt to connect those two realities.

## Start where the work happens

The first product question was not “what can AI do?” It was “what will a kitchen team actually use?”

We built the capture flow around the moment food becomes surplus: fast input, useful defaults and as little ceremony as possible. The system then has room to enrich that input with food-quality signals, carbon-footprint context and reporting.

The product now spans a mobile workflow for teams and an operational view for managers. It is being shaped through pilots that include **Sodexo and the City of Helsinki**.

## My role

As CTO, I work across the seam between product and engineering:

- turning pilot feedback into a product sequence;
- making architecture choices across the mobile app, backend and reporting surfaces;
- using AI where it removes work instead of merely adding a label;
- coordinating developers while remaining close enough to the product to build directly;
- translating restaurant reality into something a system can understand.

The stack has moved as the product has matured, but the principle has stayed stable: each technical decision should make the next real-world test easier.

## What the pilots changed

Early product thinking focused heavily on redistribution. Working with operators made the reporting layer more important. Before surplus can move intelligently, a restaurant needs to know what exists, why it exists and what keeps repeating.

That changed the shape of the product. Capture is not the destination. It is the beginning of an operating loop: **record → understand → act → learn**.

## The operating lesson

AI makes prototypes cheap. It does not make trust cheap.

The defensible part of Zipli is not that we can generate an analysis. It is that the product is learning the language, constraints and rhythms of the people who have to use it. The moat moved from the code to the closeness.
