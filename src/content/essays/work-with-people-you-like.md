Every venture of mine that actually moved, moved because there were people in it I'd have chosen to sit with anyway.

I didn't plan it that way. It's just how the men I grew up around did things. My uncle never wrote a business plan. He saw something he wanted, pulled in the people he liked, and went. The selection of people came first — before the strategy, before the spreadsheet, often before the idea was even clear. You started with *who*, and the *what* arranged itself around them. As a kid I assumed this was just how everyone operated. It took me years in a different culture to realise it was a method, and that I'd inherited a good one.

Western startup thinking treats this with suspicion. The wisdom there is to optimise for talent, for the best résumé, for the person who maximises the function regardless of whether you'd want to share a meal with them. "Don't hire your friends." "Don't mix the personal and the professional." And there's a real warning buried in that advice — liking someone is not the same as them being good, and confusing the two will hurt you. I've been burned by choosing warmth over competence. I'm not arguing for that.

I'm arguing for something more specific, which I think the optimisation crowd misses: the people you genuinely like are the only ones you'll keep showing up for when the thing stops being fun.

Because it always stops being fun. Every venture has the long middle where there's no money, no validation, no applause — just the work and the doubt. A team assembled purely on paper tends to quietly disband in that stretch, because the only thing holding it together was the upside, and the upside left. A team of people who actually like each other survives it, because there's a second reason they're in the room. They'd be in some version of that room anyway. The friendship is load-bearing exactly when the business case isn't.

This is the most underrated form of durability I know, and nobody puts it on a slide. When I think about why my food startup years ago made it as far as it did, or why Zipli keeps moving through a brutal stretch, the honest answer isn't strategy. It's that there were people beside me I'd choose to sit with regardless of outcome. Ninja at Zipli is that. The fact that I'd want to work the problem with her even if there were no company is not a soft, incidental nicety. It's the thing that keeps the company alive on the days the company gives you no reason to keep it alive.

There's an immigrant layer to this too. When you build far from home, far from the centre, you don't have the ambient infrastructure a local founder has — the warm intros, the investor who went to your school, the safety net of belonging. What you have instead, if you're from where I'm from, is a deep cultural muscle for *people as infrastructure*. Community as the thing you build on. The instinct to gather, to host, to make the people around you into the structure that holds everything up. I used to think this instinct was the unprofessional part of me, the part I had to suppress to look like a real Western founder. It's the opposite. It's my edge. The locals are better resourced. I'm better at people, because I had to be.

So I've stopped apologising for it. When I start something now, I let myself ask the question the spreadsheets don't have a column for: *do I actually want to spend the next five years with these people?* Not instead of asking whether they're good — alongside it. Because talent gets you through the demo and the people you love get you through the desert, and the desert is most of the journey.

Pick the people first. Keep the list in the glovebox. It's not the soft part of the work. On the days that matter, it's the only part holding the rest up.
