For a long time I thought I was building a company. I was actually just building.

The difference sounds small. It is the whole thing. I could prompt, design, prototype, and put something out into the world, and every time I did, it felt like I had something. A working screen. A real-looking app. A flow I could click through and show a friend. It felt like progress because it looked like progress. But no one had paid me. Nobody had even been asked to. I had mistaken the artifact for the business, the way you might mistake a very detailed drawing of a bridge for a bridge.

AI made this worse, or at least made it easier to fool myself. When building is hard, you cannot accidentally spend a year building, because the difficulty itself stops you and forces the question, is this even worth it. When building is easy, when you can conjure a polished prototype in an afternoon, nothing stops you. The ease removes the natural checkpoint. I could generate the feeling of momentum on demand. And the feeling of momentum is dangerously close to the feeling of traction, close enough that I could not tell them apart for a long time.

I also think the place I live made me more vulnerable to this. Finland is a deeply engineered, intellectual country. People here are genuinely brilliant at thinking, at service design, at mapping a problem from every angle before touching it. I absorbed that without noticing. So instead of shipping, I designed. Instead of asking someone to pay, I made another diagram of the user journey. I intellectualized, I fantasized, I evaluated, I built. I did every single activity that surrounds a business except the one activity that makes it a business, which is putting it in front of a stranger and asking them for money and seeing what happens.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to see this, and I am still not fully able to put it into words, which is part of why it was so hard to escape. The trap does not feel like a trap. It feels like diligence. It feels responsible. You tell yourself you are getting it right before you expose it. But there is no getting it right in private. The only place a product becomes right is in contact with a customer, and everything you do before that contact is rehearsal that feels like the performance.

Here is what I think is actually going on underneath. Building is safe. Shipping is not. When you build, you cannot fail, because nothing has been tested against reality yet. The prototype is always perfect in the dark. The moment you ship, the moment you ask for money, you introduce the one thing the whole elaborate process was quietly designed to avoid: the possibility of a no. So I kept building, and I called it work, and it was work, but it was the work that protects you from the verdict rather than the work that goes and gets it.

The cure is humbling and simple and I resisted it for years. Ship the embarrassing version. Ask for the money early, before it feels ready, because it will never feel ready. A single customer saying yes teaches you more than a hundred perfect prototypes, and a single customer saying no teaches you even more than that. The point was never to have something that looks like a company. The point is the payment. Everything before the payment is just a very convincing way of being afraid.

Maybe I was a little delusional. Maybe the tools just made it look too easy. But I would rather name it plainly than keep dressing it up. I spent a long time building so I would not have to ship. Now I am trying to do it the other way around.
