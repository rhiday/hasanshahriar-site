When I fly, I have a strange habit. As the plane comes down, I press my face to the window and try to match the land below to the map on my screen. Most places, it never quite lines up. The day I flew into Helsinki, it lined up perfectly — every inlet, every shape, one of them exactly like the sole of a shoe, just as the map promised. I was absurdly happy about it. I didn't know yet that I was about to spend the next several years matching a map to a territory and finding they no longer agreed.

The first thing that hit me was the silence. I'd passed through six or seven airports by then, and an airport is supposed to be a railway station on steroids — people running, luggage colliding, noise. Helsinki was empty. Not just quiet. *Empty.* There were no other passengers, no staff, glass cases in the lounge with a stuffed polar bear and a reindeer inside, and the feeling that I had walked into a country that had been left running with nobody home.

Then came the small thing I've never forgotten. At immigration, the Finns walked straight through — flash a card, gone. The foreigners stood in line. And I realised this was the exact inverse of Dhaka airport, where *we* stand in the line and the foreigners breeze past. The same scene, the roles swapped. In one direction of travel I was the local watching others queue. In the other, I was the one in the queue. Nothing about me had changed in eleven hours of flight. Everything about my position had.

I said *terve* to the bald, blue-eyed officer instead of hello, and he laughed, surprised a foreigner knew the Finnish word. Two minutes later I sat down in the main lounge, took a long breath, and the sentence arrived on its own: *I am officially an expat now.* Five thousand miles. Two years, minimum, without the people I loved. No tea-stall adda with friends. I was, at twenty-two, suddenly and permanently a person from somewhere else.

There was a second lesson waiting at the information desk, and it taught me something about the country I'd just joined. I needed a train ticket and had no card, only cash. The young man at the desk told me, pleasantly, that there was no ticket office, only card machines. I deflated. Then — only when he saw my face fall — he added, almost as an afterthought, that I could just board the train and pay the conductor in cash. He'd known the answer the whole time. He simply hadn't volunteered a word more than I'd asked for. I'd read about the Finnish habit of not saying anything extra. I didn't expect a live demonstration in my first hour.

I'm telling you all this not for the travelogue. I'm telling you because that day is when the instrument got installed, and I didn't feel it happen.

When you grow up inside one world, you never see it. It's just *the way things are* — the air, invisible. The day the line ran the other way, I started seeing the line. I started noticing the things locals couldn't, because to them the line had no direction; it was simply how airports worked. An outsider sees the shape of the room everyone else was born inside. He notices the rules are rules and not laws of physics. He notices that the same person is high-status in one airport and low-status in another, and that the difference is just geography wearing a costume.

That habit hurt for years. Noticing the water you're not supposed to be able to swim in is lonely. But it turns out to be the single most valuable perceptual habit a founder can have, and I got it for free, against my will, standing in a quiet line in a country that had been left running with nobody home. The best founders are the ones who can see something true that everyone around them has stopped seeing. Immigrants don't have to train for that. We had it done to us at the airport.

I still match the map to the territory out the window when I land. It still rarely lines up. I've stopped being upset about it. The gap between the map and the ground is exactly where I make my living now.
