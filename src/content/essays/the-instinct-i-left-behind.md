When I lived in Bangladesh, I had a stronger sense of myself. I knew what I wanted and roughly what to do about it. The instinct was loud and I trusted it. Then I immigrated, and somewhere in the move, I became more nuanced. I do not even have the right word for it. Nuanced is the closest I can get. I understood more, I saw more sides, I weighed more carefully. And I was slower, and less sure, and quieter inside.

For a while I thought this was growth. Nuance sounds like wisdom. But I have started to suspect that some of it was not wisdom at all. Some of it was hesitation wearing a nicer coat. When you arrive somewhere new and you do not yet know the rules, you learn to pause before everything. You second-guess. You check yourself against an environment you do not understand yet. That is a survival skill, and it is a good one, but it does not switch off when the danger passes. It just keeps running, and one day you notice that the loud instinct you used to have has been replaced by a committee in your head that needs to deliberate about everything.

I gained context and lost certainty. That is the trade I did not know I was making.

A few weeks ago I went looking for Hamdi Ulukaya's TED talk. I do not fully know why. And it hit me as deeply personal, more than any business talk has any right to. Not the strategy, not the numbers. The person. An immigrant who somehow kept his gut intact, who made enormous decisions from a place of conviction rather than from a committee. Watching it, I realized what I had been missing was not information. It was that. The thing he still had and I had misplaced somewhere between two countries.

And here is the part I am least proud of, because it is so obvious once you see it. When I lost my instinct, I tried to replace it with input. More podcasts. More AI. More reading about valuations and pre-seed rounds and what the smart founders are doing. As if, with enough information, I could rebuild the gut that immigrating had cost me. I was an addict for input, and I told myself it was research. But information is not instinct. You cannot read your way back to conviction. All the input did was give the committee in my head more material to argue about, while the actual thing, the first real customer paying, sat there waiting for me to stop consuming and start moving.

I think this is a specific immigrant trap, and I have not seen anyone name it. You leave a place where you knew yourself, and you arrive somewhere you have to relearn yourself, and in the relearning you accidentally outsource your judgment to everyone around you, to the books, to the experts, to the founders who seem to have it figured out. You become so good at taking in the new world that you forget you came with something. The instinct that made you brave enough to leave in the first place is still in there. You just buried it under five years of being careful.

So maybe the real work, for a founder like me, is not learning more. I have learned plenty. The real work is excavation. Getting the gut back. Trusting the version of me that existed before I had to second-guess everything just to survive a new country. The nuance can stay. It is genuinely useful. But it was supposed to be an advisor, not the decision-maker, and somewhere along the way it staged a quiet coup.

I am trying to take the chair back now. We will see.
