A few years ago, the hard part of a startup was building the thing. You needed people who could write the code, and writing the code was slow, and slowness was a filter. It kept out everyone who wasn't serious. If you had a working product, you had already crossed a moat just by existing.

That moat is mostly gone. I can describe an app out loud now and have a clickable version of it by evening. So can a teenager in Dhaka and a marketer in Berlin. When everyone can build, building is no longer where the value lives. It has quietly moved somewhere else, and most technical people are looking in the wrong direction because they're still proud of the part that stopped being scarce.

Here's where it went. It went to the boring, unglamorous things software people always looked down on. Distribution. Trust. Owning the relationship. Being the thing everyone just *defaults* to. A clever algorithm gets copied in a weekend now — someone points an AI at your product and has a rough clone by Sunday. But a clever algorithm was never much of a moat anyway. The durable ones are the unsexy ones.

Let me make it concrete, because I'm living inside this exact bet.

At Zipli, the thing we actually do is let kitchens log their food surplus. Not glamorous. A chef puts in a number of kilos at the end of the day. The "technology" is almost embarrassingly simple. But here's what's happening underneath: that kitchen now has to report those numbers to the city, for regulation. And once the chef logs it in our system, that same number can flow straight into the reporting tool the customer already uses. They stop doing the work twice. The kilos go in once and travel everywhere they need to go.

Now ask the copy-it-in-a-weekend question. Someone could clone our screens by Sunday. They cannot clone the fact that every food operator in the region has already made *us* the place the number lives. They can't clone the regulator's trust, the integration we built into the system the customer reports through, the habit of a hundred kitchens. The unsexy moat — being the default place every operator proves they followed the law — does not get copied in a weekend. It gets copied in years, if at all.

This took me a long time to accept, because I have the disease that makes you want the moat to be the *clever* part. The disease where you'd rather win on the elegant algorithm than on the boring relationship, because the algorithm feels like genius and the relationship feels like work. But the market doesn't reward what feels like genius. It rewards what's hard to take away from you.

And here is the part that turned out to be good news, at least for me.

For years I thought I had the wrong skills for this era. I'd watch real builders — the ones who could sit alone with a hard problem for ten hours and emerge with something working — and feel like a fraud, because that's not me. I'd try to grind alone at code and I'd freeze. What I'm actually good at is the other stuff. Getting people to come along. Telling the story so it lands. Being the one a room organizes around. For most of my life that felt like a consolation prize, the thing you're good at when you're not good at the real thing.

The era just turned and pointed straight at the consolation prize. The machines and the real builders are now strong exactly where I was weak, which means competing there is foolish. But they're not strong where being human, persuasive, and relentless still wins. Distribution is a human game. Trust is a human game. Showing up for the hundredth time after the others got bored is a human game. Those didn't get automated. They got *more* valuable, because everything around them got cheap.

So if you're a builder, I'd gently suggest the thing you're proudest of is no longer the thing that protects you. And if you're like me — someone who could never quite out-build the real builders — the ground just shifted under your feet in your favor. Stop apologizing for not being a machine. The machines have that covered. Go win where they can't.
