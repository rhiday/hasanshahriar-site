I have read almost everything. Clayton Christensen. The Lean Startup. Reid Hoffman. Blitzscaling. Every Paul Graham essay, some of them more than once, since I was a kid. Every YC video, every founder podcast, the LinkedIn pages, the threads. If startup knowledge could be absorbed by reading, I would have built three companies by now.

And here is the strange thing. When I actually tried to do it, as an immigrant founder, most of it was unrelatable.

I do not mean it was wrong. I mean it bounced off me. It described a country I had read about but never lived in. The books talk about co-founder disagreements and burn rate and growth curves, clean problems with clean shapes. The problems I actually had were not shaped like that. They were personal. They were cultural. They were about who I thought I was allowed to be, and what my family would think, and whether I belonged in the room at all. None of my hardest moments looked like the case studies.

Take the simplest thing nobody prepares you for: how long it takes. In my head, product-market fit was going to be quick. Find the thing people want, and then the millions, and then I would chill. That was the fantasy, and the books quietly fed it, because a book can compress a year and a half of confusion into one confident sentence. It took us about a year and a half just to find product-market fit. The sentence took me four seconds to read. Living it took eighteen months of my life.

That is the gap nobody can close for you. There is a difference between knowing a thing and feeling it, and they live in different parts of you. You can read every essay ever written about runway and still have no idea what it does to your body to wake up with an empty bank account, months of it, money that has not arrived, and no clear idea what to do today to fix it. I have now felt that. I had read all about it beforehand. The reading did not protect me even a little. Knowledge of the fire is not the same organ as the burn.

So why read at all? I have been thinking about this. I do not think the books are useless. I think they are compressed, and compression only decompresses through living. The advice is like a seed. It does nothing sitting in the packet. It only becomes real once it is planted in actual dirt, your dirt, with your weather. You read "talk to your customers" a hundred times and it means nothing, and then one day a real person pays you real money and the sentence finally explodes into meaning. The book was right. You just could not receive it yet.

The other thing I have made peace with is the timeline. There is a reason people talk about first-time founders and second-time founders like they are different species. It is just a game, like any other. You start as a beginner. You play badly, you lose, you slowly stop making the obvious mistakes, and you go up a level. I used to find that framing slightly insulting, as if my reading should have let me skip ahead. It does not let you skip ahead. Nobody skips the beginner levels. You can read every strategy guide for a game you have never played and you will still lose your first ten matches, because the guide is information and the game is reflex, and reflex is only built by playing.

I am still not entirely sure why I am doing this. That is an honest sentence and I am going to leave it in. But I have stopped expecting the journey to be short, and I have stopped feeling cheated that the books did not match my life. They were not written for my life. They were maps, and maps are useful, but the map was drawn for a different country than the one I am walking through. The walking is the only part that was ever going to teach me.

And I think we are only at the beginning of it. Let us see how the next stage goes.
